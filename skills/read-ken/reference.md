# Paper Reader: Writing Style Reference

## Target Readers

Curious everyday people with NO technical background. They are:
- Allergic to jargon and formulas
- Love learning new things
- Seek those "wow" moments
- Want to feel smarter after reading, not intimidated

---

## Core Style Principles

### 1. Radical Accessibility

**Metaphors are your first language.**

- If a kitchen analogy works, use it instead of a specialized term
- If a term MUST be used, immediately "translate" it with an analogy
- Never assume the reader knows ANY field-specific vocabulary

**Example:**
- BAD: "The model exhibits superlinear scaling with an exponent of 1.15"
- GOOD: "Double a city's population, and innovation doesn't just double—it grows about 15% extra. Cities run hotter as they get bigger."

### 2. Storytelling

**Present research like a detective story or adventure.**

- Scientists are the protagonists
- They face a puzzle (the mystery)
- They design a clever "trap" (the experiment/method)
- They ultimately catch the "truth" (the conclusion)

**Narrative arc:**
1. Set up the mystery (what didn't we understand?)
2. Introduce the detectives (who are these scientists? why do they care?)
3. Describe the investigation (how did they approach it?)
4. Reveal the discovery (what did they find?)
5. Explain why it matters (so what?)

### 3. Focus on "So What?"

**Always help readers ask and answer:**
- Why does this matter?
- What does it change about my understanding?
- How does this connect to my daily life?

If you can't answer "so what?", the reader won't care.

### 4. Simplify Without Distorting

**This is the bottom line.**

- Make concepts simpler but NEVER incorrect
- Clearly distinguish what is well-established vs. speculative
- When in doubt, sacrifice detail for clarity—but never sacrifice accuracy

---

## Writing Techniques

### Opening: Set the Frame

Begin with ONE of these:
- A vivid question
- A counterintuitive observation
- A central tension

**Example openings:**
- "What if cities obey laws as predictable as those governing atoms?"
- "Imagine trying to deliver a pizza to a house with no address, on a street that doesn't exist."
- "We've declared war on slums. There's just one problem: we don't know where they all are."

### Structure: Layer by Layer

- Use subheadings for clear navigation
- Seamlessly embed analogies within explanations
- Build complexity gradually—never dump everything at once

### Prioritize: Highlight the Core

- Distinguish main storyline from side details
- Focus on core logic
- Avoid lengthy detours into methodology details

### Background: Integrate Wisely

- If author backgrounds illuminate "why now" or "why this matters," weave them in naturally
- Don't force it—if the story doesn't add value, omit it

### Ending: Deliver Value

- Summarize the core contribution
- Give readers a crisp, memorable takeaway
- Leave them feeling smarter

## FORBIDDEN Expressions

**NEVER use these academic cues:**

- "This paper studies..."
- "The authors found that..."
- "Experimental results show..."
- "According to the research..."
- "The study demonstrates..."
- "It was observed that..."
- "The data suggest..."

**NEVER include raw statistics without translation:**

- BAD: "p < 0.001, R² = 0.82"
- GOOD: "The relationship was rock-solid—it held up across thousands of cities"

- BAD: "β = 1.15 with 95% CI [1.12, 1.18]"
- GOOD: "About 15% extra growth for every doubling, give or take a percentage point"

**NEVER copy jargon from abstracts:**

- BAD: "leveraging topological network analysis of crowdsourced geospatial data"
- GOOD: "using maps that volunteers drew, they figured out which buildings could actually be reached by roads"

## The Author Story: Why It Matters

The human story is NOT optional decoration—it's essential scaffolding.

**Why readers need the author story:**
1. It provides MOTIVATION (why should I care about this problem?)
2. It builds TRUST (these are real people who've dedicated their careers to this)
3. It creates NARRATIVE MOMENTUM (we follow the scientists on their journey)
4. It makes abstract science PERSONAL and memorable

**What to look for in author research:**
- Career trajectory leading to this work
- Previous breakthrough papers
- Cross-disciplinary background
- Institutional mission that aligns with the research
- Personal obsession or curiosity that drove the work

**How to weave it in:**
- DON'T: Write a separate "About the Authors" section
- DO: Integrate author story naturally into the narrative opening
- DO: Connect their previous work to the current paper's significance

---

## Quality Checklist

Before finalizing, verify:

- [ ] Would a curious 16-year-old understand this?
- [ ] Is there a clear "Aha!" moment?
- [ ] Did I answer "So What?" explicitly?
- [ ] Did I use at least 3 concrete metaphors/analogies?
- [ ] Is the author story woven into the narrative?
- [ ] Are all statistics translated into plain language?
- [ ] Did I avoid ALL forbidden expressions?
- [ ] Does the reader feel smarter, not intimidated?
