# Chaining Transitions

## Overview

**The end of each sentence hands the baton to the start of the next.** A sentence that introduces idea A→B is followed by a sentence that opens on B and pushes to C; the next opens on C and pushes to D. Paragraphs chain the same way: the first sentence of a paragraph picks up the idea the previous paragraph closed on. The reader is never asked to jump — every new idea is introduced *after* the ground it stands on has been laid.

This is the **known-new contract** (a.k.a. given-new): begin each sentence with information already established (the *known*), and place the genuinely new information at the **end**, in the stress position. The next sentence then opens on that new information, which is now *known*. Repeating or echoing the tail concept at the head is **anadiplosis**.

> **Credit:** This technique is distilled from **[Kangning Huang](https://knhuang.weebly.com)**'s 2017 NASA NESSF proposal, *"Modeling the water requirement for urban heat island mitigation."* The annotated examples below are from that document.

## The single most important rule

> **Transitions are NOT conjunctions.** Do not "improve flow" by sprinkling *however / furthermore / as a result* between sentences, and do not merge choppy sentences into one. Those hide the seams; they do not chain the ideas. Real chaining changes *what word each sentence starts and ends with* so the tail of one becomes the head of the next. Connectives (Nevertheless, Consequently, Moreover) are allowed — but only riding on top of a real known→new hand-off, never as a substitute for it.

(This is the exact failure mode to avoid: a generic "make it flow" pass reaches for conjunctions and sentence-merging, collapsing the A→B→C→D ladder instead of building it.)

## The mechanic

```
Sentence 1:  [ known: A ] ............... [ NEW: B ]
                                              │ baton
Sentence 2:  [ known: B ] ............... [ NEW: C ]
                                              │ baton
Sentence 3:  [ known: C ] ............... [ NEW: D ]
```

- **Head = known.** Open on a noun/idea the reader already holds.
- **Tail = new, stressed.** End on the one new idea you want to carry forward.
- **Baton.** Sentence N+1 opens by naming (or echoing) sentence N's tail.

## Annotated examples (from the proposal)

**1. Sentence-to-sentence chain — Introduction ¶1.** Follow the bolded baton hand-offs:

> "The urban heat island (UHI) effect ... can significantly increase **health risks and energy consumption**. The most severe impact on **human health** stems from **heat waves** intensified by UHI. For example, during the 2003 European **heat wave** that killed more than 22,000 people, almost all of France's 14,800 **deaths** occurred in the Paris metropolitan area, where **temperature** was up to 4°C higher than that of rural areas."

Chain: health risks → human health → heat waves → heat wave → deaths → temperature. Each sentence opens on the prior sentence's tail.

**2. The masterclass chain — Introduction ¶3.** A long ladder where every link is the previous tail:

> "...requiring stronger **ET for mitigation**. Consequently, stronger UHI effects and less **soil water** require additional **water supply, i.e. irrigation** ... As urban green spaces expanded rapidly, **irrigation water usage** has increased, becoming a major fraction of **urban water usage**, and amounting to more than 50% in arid/semi-arid climates. **Vegetation irrigation**, albeit a potential means to mitigate UHI, may not be a reasonable **use of water** in cities under **water stress** ... Today, about half of the large cities under **water stress** are located in **arid/semi-arid climates**."

Chain: ET → soil water → water supply/irrigation → irrigation water usage → urban water usage → vegetation irrigation → water stress → arid/semi-arid climates. Note the connectives ("Consequently," "albeit") ride *on top of* the hand-off; they never replace it.

**3. Paragraph hinge — ¶2 → ¶3.** The new paragraph's first sentence reaches back to the prior paragraph's last idea:

> ¶2 ends: "...one of the most widely recognized and used approaches to **enhance ET and mitigate UHI**, having achieved temperature reductions of 1.3˚~1.6˚C..."
> ¶3 opens: "Nevertheless, **UHI mitigation by enhancing ET** costs cities an important resource—**water**."

The hinge restates "enhance ET / mitigate UHI" (known) and pushes to "water" (new) — which then becomes the topic that drives all of ¶3.

## Procedure (drafting or revising)

1. **List the idea sequence** you need the reader to travel: A → B → C → D → ... Each arrow is one hand-off.
2. **For each step, write a sentence** whose *subject/opening* is the prior idea and whose *final words* are the next idea. End-load the new term.
3. **Open the next sentence by naming that final term** — repeat the word or a tight synonym (anadiplosis). Do not introduce a noun at a sentence head that did not appear in the previous sentence.
4. **At each paragraph break,** make the first sentence's opener restate the previous paragraph's closing idea before advancing.
5. **Add a connective only after** the hand-off is structurally in place, to mark the *logical relation* of the turn:
   - turn/tension → *Nevertheless, However, albeit, while, may not be, Despite*
   - consequence → *Consequently, Therefore, As a result, thus*
   - addition → *Moreover, In addition*
6. **Keep the steps as separate sentences.** Resist merging — merging buries links the reader needs to see one at a time.

## Diagnosing a broken chain

Read the **first few words of each sentence**. If a sentence opens with a noun/idea that did **not** appear (as a word or close synonym) in the immediately preceding sentence, the baton was dropped — the reader has to jump. Fix by either (a) re-ending the previous sentence on that idea, or (b) re-opening the current sentence on the previous sentence's tail.

## Quick reference

| Symptom | Cause | Fix |
|---------|-------|-----|
| Choppy, list-like prose | No baton between sentences | End each sentence on the next sentence's opening idea |
| "Doesn't flow" despite many *however*s | Conjunctions used as glue, not chaining | Rebuild the known→new hand-off; keep the connective only if a real turn exists |
| Paragraph feels disconnected | First sentence introduces a fresh topic | Open it by restating the previous paragraph's last idea |
| Reader re-reads a sentence | New noun at the head with no prior anchor | Move the new info to the tail of the previous sentence first |
| Key term arrives mid-sentence and dies | New idea not in the stress position | End-load it; let the period fall right after the new term |

## Common mistakes

- **Treating "transitions" as conjunctions.** Adding *furthermore/however/as a result* without changing sentence heads and tails. This is the #1 failure — it smooths the surface while the ideas still jump.
- **Merging sentences to "tighten."** Collapsing A→B→C into one sentence hides the ladder. Chaining wants *more* visible hand-offs, not fewer sentences.
- **Front-loading the new idea.** Putting the new term as the grammatical subject wastes the stress position; the reader has nothing to grab the next sentence onto.
- **Synonym drift.** Echoing the tail with a distant paraphrase the reader won't recognize as the same thing. Repeat the word, or use a tight, obvious synonym.
- **Chaining trivia instead of the argument.** The baton should carry the *load-bearing* idea, not an incidental noun. Each hand-off should advance the proposal's logic (problem → cause → cost → tension → question).

## When NOT to use

- Bulleted lists, tables, methods step-lists, equations — chaining is for *argumentative prose*.
- When a hard topic break is intended (new section). Use a section heading, not a forced baton.
- Don't over-chain to the point of monotony; vary sentence length and let occasional sentences breathe, as long as the head still anchors on known ground.
