# Worked Examples: Before and After

## Example 1: Urban Scaling Paper

### Paper Abstract (Original)
"We propose a simple spatial attraction mechanism that combines competition between population aggregation and exploration of new areas in a city to determine the spatial distribution of an active population. Our model successfully reproduces the spatial distributions of population, road networks, and socioeconomic interactions, and reveals that their spatial scalings are in a consistent framework."

### Academic Summary (Wrong Approach)
"This paper proposes a spatial attraction model for urban growth. The model has one free parameter C that controls the competition between population aggregation and spatial exploration. The authors derive power-law relationships for population density ρ(r) ∝ r^(-β), road length l(r) ∝ ρ^(1/2), and socioeconomic output g(r) ∝ ρ^(3/2). They validate these predictions against data from London and Beijing..."

### Gladwell-Style Opening (Right Approach)
"In 1854, a physician named John Snow did something peculiar. Instead of treating cholera patients in London, he started drawing dots on a map. Each dot represented a death. When he finished, the pattern was unmistakable: the deaths clustered around a single water pump on Broad Street.

A century and a half later, a physicist named Ruiqi Li sat in a lab at Beijing Normal University, staring at a different kind of map. This one showed Greater London from space at night, the city glowing like a neural network, brightest at the center, dimming toward the edges. Li wasn't looking for disease. He was looking for something stranger: a mathematical formula that could predict exactly how bright any given kilometer of that city should be, using nothing more than a head count..."

### Why It Works
- Opens with a character (John Snow), not a concept
- Creates a mystery (what is Li looking for?)
- Delays the technical content
- Uses concrete imagery (glowing neural network)
- Establishes stakes implicitly (prediction at km-level)

## Example 2: Psychology Finding

### Original Finding
"Results showed that participants who were given 24 choices of jam were less likely to make a purchase (3%) compared to those given 6 choices (30%)."

### Academic Summary (Wrong)
"Iyengar and Lepper (2000) demonstrated that extensive choice can demotivate consumer behavior. In a field experiment at a supermarket, they found that..."

### Gladwell-Style Treatment (Right)
"The supermarket in Menlo Park, California had set up a tasting booth. One day, shoppers could sample from 24 varieties of jam. Another day, just 6. Here's what happened.

The big display attracted more people. Obviously—more options, more curiosity. But when researchers tracked who actually bought jam, the pattern reversed. At the small display, 30% of tasters purchased. At the large display? Three percent.

Put another way: you could increase your sales tenfold by offering *fewer* options..."

### Why It Works
- Concrete scene (supermarket, tasting booth)
- Numeric contrast creates surprise
- Short paragraphs build rhythm
- Ends with practical implication

## Example 3: Technical Mechanism

### Original
"The spatial distribution follows ρ(r) ∝ r^(-β), where β is derived from the parameter C controlling the trade-off between aggregation and exploration."

### Wrong Approach
"The population density decays as a power law with exponent β, which is determined by the model parameter C that represents the relative attraction of populated versus empty areas."

### Right Approach
"Think of a city as a party that's been going on for centuries. Early guests cluster in the center—that's where the action is. But as more people arrive, some gravitate toward the crowd, while others drift to quieter corners where there's room to move.

A single number captures this tug-of-war: call it β. When β is high, everyone's at the center, packed shoulder to shoulder. When β is low, the party spreads across the entire venue.

Li discovered you can measure β from satellite photos. London's is 0.30—steep, concentrated. Beijing's is 0.09—flat, sprawling. The number doesn't just describe where people are. It predicts where the roads will go, and where the money will flow."

### Why It Works
- Analogy (party) makes abstract concrete
- Avoids equation entirely while preserving meaning
- Uses specific numbers (0.30, 0.09) for credibility
- Ends with implications, not definition

## Common Transformations

| Academic Phrasing | Gladwell Alternative |
|-------------------|----------------------|
| "Results indicate that..." | "Here's what happened..." |
| "This finding suggests..." | "Which means..." |
| "Consistent with prior research..." | "Other scientists had noticed something similar..." |
| "We hypothesized that..." | "They had a hunch..." |
| "The data revealed..." | "The pattern was unmistakable..." |
| "Significant at p < 0.05" | "The effect was real—not a fluke of the data" |
| "Further research is needed" | "One question remains unanswered" |

## Structural Template

```
[HOOK: Character, scene, or contradiction - 100-200 words]

---

[CONTEXT: Why this matters, what we thought we knew - 200-300 words]

---

[THE FINDING: Core insight, delayed reveal - 300-500 words]

---

[THE MECHANISM: How it works, made intuitive - 300-500 words]

---

[IMPLICATIONS: So what? For whom? - 200-300 words]

---

[CLOSE: Return to opening, transformed - 100-200 words]
```

This is a guideline, not a formula. Break it when the material demands.
