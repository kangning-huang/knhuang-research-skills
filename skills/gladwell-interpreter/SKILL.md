# Gladwell Paper Interpreter

Transform academic papers into narratives that read like the opening chapter of a book someone would buy in an airport.

## Core Method

### 1. Find the Human Door
Never open with the abstract. Find a person, moment, or scene—a researcher's obsession, a subject's unexpected behavior, a historical figure who stumbled onto the same question. Drop the reader into specificity before revealing the topic.

### 2. Delay the Thesis
Build curiosity before summarizing. The main insight can wait. Let tension accumulate until everything clicks. The reader should wonder "where is this going?" until they suddenly see it.

### 3. Translate Jargon into Intuition
Don't define technical terms—*show* them. Describe what "cognitive load" feels like before naming it. The reader should grasp concepts viscerally before learning terminology.

### 4. Find the Counterintuitive Angle
Gladwell's signature: "We assumed X, but what if the opposite is true?" Dig for findings that challenge common sense. If the paper confirms convention, find the surprising mechanism underneath.

### 5. Braid Multiple Threads
Weave seemingly unrelated stories that converge on the same insight. A paper about hospital errors might connect to airline pilots and basketball coaches. Find the rhymes across domains.

### 6. Make Stakes Visceral
Not "this matters for policy" but: Who gets hurt if we're wrong? Who benefits if we're right? Ground implications in real consequences readers can feel.

### 7. End with Resonance
No recap. End with an image, a question, or a return to the opening—now transformed by everything learned. Leave readers thinking.

## Voice Guidelines

- Present tense for scenes and studies
- Short paragraphs, varied sentence length
- Rhetorical questions—sparingly, strategically
- Confident but never condescending
- Embrace "turns out" when revealing findings
- Use "you" to place reader inside experience
- Section breaks (---) to shift scenes, not headers

## What to Avoid

- Bullet points, headers, academic structure
- "This paper examines..." or abstract-style openings
- Hedge words that drain energy ("somewhat," "arguably")
- Explaining *why* something is interesting—just make it interesting
- Treating researchers as invisible; they're characters

## Process

1. **First read**: Identify the core finding and why it matters
2. **Find the hook**: What person, moment, or scene offers entry?
3. **Map the arc**: What's the journey from hook to insight?
4. **Identify threads**: What analogies, historical parallels, or cross-domain connections illuminate the idea?
5. **Draft**: Write as continuous prose, not sections
6. **Refine**: Cut every sentence that doesn't earn its place

## Handling Technical Content

For equations, methods, or statistical results:
- Translate to intuition first, precision second
- Use concrete examples before abstractions
- It's acceptable to simplify—accuracy of *understanding* matters more than accuracy of *detail*
- When precision is critical, weave it into narrative rather than presenting it as exposition

## Length Calibration

- Quick explanation: 800–1,200 words
- Standard interpretation: 1,500–2,500 words
- Deep dive: 3,000–5,000 words

Match length to paper complexity and user need. When in doubt, favor the shorter version that preserves narrative momentum.
